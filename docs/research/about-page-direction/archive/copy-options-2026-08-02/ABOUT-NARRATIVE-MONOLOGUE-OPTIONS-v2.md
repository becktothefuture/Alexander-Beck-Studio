# About narrative: three purpose-led monologue options v2

Status: revised review directions

Prepared: 2026-08-02

These versions replace the earlier continuous-versus-skimmable comparison. They share the same animation, topic order, client proof, discipline reveal, project impressions and finale actions. Each now tests a different editorial strategy:

1. **Continuous prose** — a concise account of the career journey and present philosophy.
2. **Career milestones and ways of working** — bullets are reserved for the two things a reader may need to scan and remember.
3. **Personal, reflective and informational** — career facts are carried by a more recognisable personal voice.
4. **Extended long-form** — a genuinely longer, slower account that gives the transitions and their consequences room to develop.

The purpose of every version is to introduce Alex as a person, make the development of his practice credible, show what it feels like to work with him, and create interest in what he might make next. The page is not a manifesto, a complete CV or a catalogue of capabilities.

## Version 4 — extended long-form

### Opener

**Title**

> About Me

**Description**

> I’m Alex, a designer whose practice has grown across visual communication, digital products, systems and emerging technology—usually where a problem needs more than one kind of thinking.

### Early travelling titles

> I didn’t begin with a plan to work across so many disciplines…

> …I kept following the parts of each problem I still needed to understand.

### First reading block

> Hi, I’m Alex. I’m a designer at heart, and I love the moment when something difficult begins to take a form that other people can see, question and make their own.

> I began by studying Computer Science. I was fascinated by what technology could do, but speaking several languages had also made me attentive to the way meaning changes between people and contexts. I became increasingly interested in the relationship between a system and the person trying to understand it. Moving into Communication Design in Mainz brought those interests together: language, technology, images and the careful work of making an idea legible.

> My early agency work was rooted in print and culture, where composition, typography and a strong visual idea carried the message. Digital work added another dimension. Interfaces had to behave, icons had to explain themselves and motion could reveal how a system worked. Working closely with developers taught me that a design does not end when it looks resolved. Moving to London for Yoti made that even clearer. Product, brand and communication all had to help people understand and trust an unfamiliar identity technology. Since then, agency and independent work have taken me further into design systems, implementation, motion, 3D and AI—not as separate interests, but as different ways to answer the problem in front of me.

`[Selected work from across my career · client logo grid]`

### Bridge travelling titles

> The work became broader because the problems kept asking for more…

> …but breadth only matters when it helps people understand what is in front of them.

`[Shared discipline reveal]`

### Second reading block

> Many projects arrive as a tidy brief wrapped around a much less tidy situation. Before deciding what to make, I try to understand that situation properly. I listen to the people closest to it, look for the assumptions everyone has learned to work around and ask what the brief may have left unsaid. Complexity is not something I want to decorate or flatten. The useful work is finding a clear way through it without pretending that its important tensions have disappeared.

> That understanding becomes most useful when it is made tangible early. A sketch, prototype, motion study or piece of code gives a team something real to react to while the important choices are still open. I enjoy giving a project direction, but I do not want to protect an idea from being challenged. The best collaborations are generous and direct: people feel heard, difficult decisions become shared decisions and I stay close enough to implementation to help the original thought survive contact with reality.

> We are also living through a period when technology changes faster than our shared understanding of it. That is exciting, and sometimes unsettling. AI lets me explore unfamiliar territory, connect ideas and prototype much faster than before. It can also make it very easy to produce polished copies of familiar things. I want to use it purposefully: to extend human thinking rather than replace it, and to create work that still carries judgement, responsibility, character and a visible human footprint.

`[Project impressions]`

> The same curiosity follows me outside commissioned work. It appears in music, Lego and independent games, and in small frustrations that become difficult to leave alone—including an attempt to redesign the iOS keyboard. I keep returning to questions around trust, privacy, robotics and the way unfamiliar systems enter everyday life. Side projects give me room to stay with those questions before anyone knows exactly what the answer should look like.

### Closing travelling titles

> I’m still looking for questions that don’t arrive with their form already decided…

> …questions that need patience, imagination and several kinds of thinking…

> …and people willing to make a new answer together.

### Finale

**Title**

> Let’s talk.

**Description**

> If you’re working through something useful, unfamiliar or difficult to explain, I’d be glad to hear where the problem began—and where you hope it could lead.
