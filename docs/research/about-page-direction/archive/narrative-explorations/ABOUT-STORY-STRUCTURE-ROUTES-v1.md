# About Me story: three writing routes

## How to read this document

These are early voice and content explorations, not the canonical page structure. The evergreen dramatic architecture, section contracts, information allocation and population worksheet now live in `ABOUT-NARRATIVE-FRAMEWORK.md`.

The framework establishes a five-act transformation:

**complexity → attention → structure → character → possibility**

It also changes the job of the first section. The opening is a **threshold and story promise**, not necessarily a personal hook. These routes are therefore useful source material for specificity, cadence and proof, but their opening lines should be reconsidered when the next story draft is populated from the framework.

The reusable writing rules remain in `ABOUT-TONE-OF-VOICE.md`. The background remains an emotional counterpoint; the complete story must still work in a plain text document.

## Route A: Learning to look

**Angle:** Literary, observant and craft-led. The recurring idea is attention: learning to see how visual decisions change meaning.

### 1. Spatial — Threshold experiment

> I learned design by learning to look.
>
> Composition. Form. Shape. What changes when one element moves.

### 2. Editorial — Personal lens

Composition, form, shape, typography, image and sequence gave me a broad visual foundation. I studied Communication Design in Mainz, where the exercises changed constantly but the lesson stayed with me: small decisions can completely alter what something communicates and how it feels.

I still keep some of that early work around: typographic studies, image experiments, the occasional poster and things that never fitted a neat category. They are useful reminders that precision and personality can occupy the same piece of work.

### 3. Spatial — Expansion

> The canvas changed.
>
> The same attention moved into products, services and systems.

### 4. Editorial — Present practice

Today I work across product design, experience design and art direction for international clients, including the American Heart Association, S&P Global, Bentley, Yoti and SunExpress.

Depending on the problem, that can mean shaping a journey, building a design system, prototyping an interaction, using motion to guide attention, or writing code to make an idea tangible. I also use AI-generated imagery when it helps me take a visual beyond the obvious and give the final image more precision and character.

The projects change, but the question stays surprisingly close to those first visual exercises: how do you make something clear without making it anonymous? I like finding the answer with a team, testing it early and carrying the strongest idea through the details.

### 5. Spatial — Open ending

> Bring me the problem that has not found its form yet.
>
> Let’s see what it can become.

## Route B: Work that crosses disciplines

**Angle:** Direct, contemporary and commercially legible. It opens with temperament rather than chronology and makes Alexander’s breadth feel purposeful.

### 1. Spatial — Threshold experiment

> I’m happiest when a project refuses to fit neatly into one discipline.

### 2. Editorial — Personal lens

My foundation was visual: learning how composition, form, shape, type and image direct attention. University gave me room to test those principles across different kinds of work. The important lesson was learning how to make choices visible: what deserves attention, what can disappear, and where one unexpected detail can change the whole thing.

That training still shapes how I work. The medium has changed; the appetite for clarity, character and surprise has not.

### 3. Spatial — Expansion

> Then the decisions became connected.
>
> It moved into products, services and systems.

### 4. Editorial — Present practice

Today I work with international clients across product design, experience design, systems design and art direction. My projects have moved through health, finance, identity, automotive and travel, with organisations including the American Heart Association, S&P Global, Bentley, Yoti and SunExpress.

I bring disciplines together around the problem. A prototype can make an idea testable. Motion can guide attention. A design system can keep hundreds of decisions coherent. Visual delight can give someone a reason to look twice. AI image generation is another part of that toolkit when it helps push atmosphere, texture and detail beyond the expected.

Whatever the medium, it has to work. It should also feel like someone cared about the details. The most interesting projects need both: a system strong enough to hold together and a point of view specific enough to be remembered.

## Route C: The questions grew

**Angle:** Performable, cumulative and emotionally direct. It has the clearest dramatic curve and the strongest explicit turn between usefulness and feeling.

### 1. Spatial — Threshold experiment

> My earliest design lessons were simple.
>
> Where something sits. What it weighs. What changes when it moves.

### 2. Editorial — Personal lens

I started with the fundamentals of visual communication: composition, form, shape, type, image and space. I explored them through many different formats and experiments.

The formats changed, but the lesson was consistent: every choice changes the experience.

### 3. Spatial — Expansion

> A composition became an interaction.
>
> An interaction became a journey.
>
> A journey opened into a system.

### 4. Editorial — Present practice and turn

The scale changed, and so did the responsibility. Today I collaborate with international clients on products, services and digital brands. That has included the American Heart Association, S&P Global, Bentley, Yoti and SunExpress.

My practice moves between experience design, systems design, art direction, prototyping, motion and creative engineering. I use each where it adds something the others cannot: a prototype to make an idea testable, motion to guide attention, a system to hold the decisions together, and visual delight to make the result worth returning to. AI image generation has become part of that toolkit too, helping me push atmosphere, texture and detail further without replacing judgement.

I care whether something works. I care how it feels to use. I have never seen a reason to choose. The interesting work happens when structure gives creativity something to push against, and creativity gives the system character.

## Recommendation

Use **Route C’s dramatic curve** as the structural benchmark, **Route A’s observant specificity** for the visual foundation, and **Route B’s commercial clarity** when describing the present practice.

The strongest phrase from the current route openings is:

> I learned design by learning to look.

It belongs more naturally in the personal-lens section than at the threshold. The next draft should test it after a premise-led opening rather than asking it to carry the whole story promise.

The current framework benchmark for the threshold is:

> The hardest part of making something clear is deciding what not to lose.

This is a statement of function, not locked public copy. It creates the question that the personal lens, professional expansion and present practice must answer.

The strongest central idea is:

> I care whether something works. I care how it feels to use. I have never seen a reason to choose.

It says what the earlier “clarity without flattening” argument was trying to say, but in plain, personal language. It also works without any animation.

The strongest ending should be an invitation, not a slogan:

> Have something new in mind? Tell me the complicated part. Let’s build from there.

## What changes in the next storyboard

The storyboard should no longer be organised around explaining a cloud, grid or heartbeat. It should be organised around these five story jobs. The background states are then assigned as emotional counterpoints:

1. anticipation;
2. concentration;
3. expansion;
4. complexity held in balance;
5. release and openness.

That keeps the text layer calm and makes every major animation moment answer a different emotional need.
