# Portfolio Project Content Structure

## Factual source of truth

This document defines editorial structure, not project facts. Final copy must be derived from confirmed, source-linked claims in `docs/portfolio/projects/` and must pass the copy gate in `docs/portfolio/catalog.json`.

## Purpose

Each project should work at two speeds:

- a recruiter can understand fit, seniority, and contribution in under a minute;
- a design leader, founder, or CEO can keep reading and find judgment, craft, collaboration, and honest evidence.

The portfolio should not turn six different pieces of work into six copies of the same case study. Use one dependable reading frame, then give every project its own central question and editorial rhythm.

## The fixed frame

These elements stay consistent so the reader never has to relearn the interface.

### 1. Opening view

- Client and project title
- One-sentence premise written around the real tension, not a generic achievement
- Strong opening image or short muted video
- Small metadata rail

The premise should sound specific enough that it could not be moved to another project unchanged.

### 2. Metadata rail

Use only fields that help someone judge the work:

- **My role** — the role actually performed, not only the job title
- **Team** — disciplines and close collaborators; name people when permission allows
- **Time** — year or honest range, plus duration when known
- **Status** — shipped, live, internal, concept, archive, or ongoing
- **Scope** — product, service, campaign, platform, or system boundary
- **Disciplines** — three to five concrete capabilities
- **Hands-on** — the things personally designed, made, prototyped, facilitated, or directed
- **Constraints** — one short phrase when it materially shaped the work

Client confidentiality should be stated plainly. Do not turn missing details into vague language.

### 3. Short introduction: What / Why / Me / With

Write 80–120 words in natural prose. It should answer:

- What was being made or changed?
- Why did it matter at that moment?
- What was I personally responsible for?
- Who did I work with?

This replaces the usual long project background. If the reader stops here, they should still understand the project.

### 4. The project story

Choose three to five blocks from the block library below. Do not use every block and do not keep the same order across projects.

Every story needs to reveal:

- one consequential decision;
- one constraint, disagreement, or awkward reality;
- one example of collaboration;
- enough final work to judge visual and interaction craft;
- one honest reflection.

### 5. What changed

Close the story with evidence, not theatre. Use verified metrics when they exist. When they do not, describe observable change:

- a manual workflow became a shared product flow;
- a decision was made or a direction was adopted;
- a system, prototype, model, or language became usable by other people;
- a flow shipped, was tested, or reached implementation;
- a team gained clarity, consistency, or a way to continue the work;
- the work exposed a limitation and changed the next decision.

Separate **shipped outcome**, **team outcome**, and **what remains unknown** when that distinction matters.

### 6. Honest ending

Finish with 60–100 words under a project-specific heading. Good endings include what would be done differently, what did not survive, what remains unproven, or what the project changed in the designer's practice.

Then show credits, relevant links, and a bottom **Back to portfolio** control.

## Variable block library

Headings should be written for the story rather than copied from this list.

- **The knot** — the contradiction or difficult part that made the project interesting
- **What I noticed** — an observation that changed the brief or direction
- **A decision worth showing** — two or three credible options and why one won
- **The thing we had to make visible** — a model, rule, risk, or system users could not previously see
- **Inside the working room** — collaborator roles, disagreement, feedback, and how a decision moved
- **The constraint that helped** — technical, regulatory, brand, time, data, or organisational limits
- **The constraint I pushed back on** — what was challenged, how, and with what result
- **Where craft mattered** — close-ups of hierarchy, behaviour, motion, type, iconography, or system detail
- **The handoff** — how an idea moved between devices, teams, channels, or implementation states
- **What did not make it** — a rejected direction or lost battle with a useful reason
- **What shipped versus what was designed** — a transparent comparison when the two differ
- **The afterlife** — how the work was used, maintained, extended, or learned from
- **What I would test next** — a real unanswered question, not a ceremonial conclusion

## Project-specific story directions

The titles below are working editorial directions, not final copy.

### S&P Global — Making rules behave like objects

Lead with the translation problem: financial logic lived across email, spreadsheets, and scripts. Build the story around the modular interaction model, the hardest rule or validation decision, and how technical and non-technical collaborators came to share the same mental model. End with what became faster or clearer only if the evidence can be verified.

Suggested blocks: **The thing we had to make visible**, **A decision worth showing**, **Inside the working room**, **What changed**.

### Bentley — Designing restraint

Treat this as a story about judgment and tone rather than a tour of polished screens. Show how brand character became pacing, motion, hierarchy, and interaction decisions. Include one moment where a more expressive direction was reduced or rejected. Make the hands-on 3D and prototyping work unmistakable without claiming sole authorship of the wider system.

Suggested blocks: **What I noticed**, **Where craft mattered**, **What did not make it**, **The afterlife**.

### Yoti — Earning the next tap

Frame the project around trust at sensitive moments. Follow one verification journey across devices and expose the reasons behind copy, waiting, recovery, and feedback decisions. Show where regulation, technology, and user anxiety pulled in different directions, plus who helped resolve those tensions.

Suggested blocks: **The knot**, **The handoff**, **The constraint that helped**, **What I would test next**.

### SunExpress — What still holds up

Keep the archive status visible and make that honesty the device. Briefly place the visual work in its period, then concentrate on the durable decisions: multilingual booking, transactional hierarchy, accessibility, iconography, and a system tested against real booking cases. Name what has aged and what remains good practice.

Suggested blocks: **What aged**, **The decision that still holds**, **Inside the working room**, **What I would now change**.

### Personal website — The portfolio as a live material

Use a lab-notebook rhythm rather than a client case-study rhythm. Alternate finished moments with experiments, browser constraints, performance findings, and iterations. Show that design and implementation are one continuous practice here. The imperfections and version changes are part of the evidence.

Suggested blocks: **The question I kept testing**, **A version that failed**, **Where code changed the design**, **What is still moving**.

### Pro Keyboard — A small idea under test

Do not inflate the concept into a shipped-product story. Begin with the learning behaviour being explored, show the smallest useful loop, and distinguish design belief from evidence. Its value is focus, not scale. If there is not enough real material for a ten-item story, expand the project through genuine research, prototypes, states, and testing rather than repeated mockups.

Suggested blocks: **The hunch**, **The smallest useful loop**, **What remains unproven**, **The next test**.

## Media rhythm: at least ten useful items

Ten is a floor for editorial depth, not a reason to repeat the same mockup. A useful sequence is:

1. Opening image or short motion piece
2. Context or original landscape
3. Evidence of the problem
4. Model, map, or early framing
5. Rejected or competing direction
6. Key interaction sequence
7. System or craft close-up
8. Collaboration, prototype, or working artefact
9. Final outcome in context
10. Implementation, use, comparison, or reflective coda

Images can work singly, as pairs, or in short sequences. Give each a short caption that explains what the reader should notice. Avoid device-mockup wallpaper, repeated hero crops, and process artefacts included only to prove that a method happened.

## Writing rules

- Target roughly 450–700 words per project, excluding captions and metadata.
- Let images carry the visual explanation; use text for decisions, tensions, and context the image cannot reveal.
- Prefer concrete nouns and verbs. Name the screen, rule, conversation, prototype, or constraint.
- Use **I** for personal work and **we** only when the team's contribution is clear nearby.
- Credit collaborators without shrinking personal responsibility.
- Include one detail that could only come from being there.
- Admit uncertainty, compromise, or missing evidence when it is true.
- Vary sentence length and section rhythm. Not every paragraph needs a lesson.
- Avoid claims such as “seamless,” “innovative,” “user-centric,” “end-to-end,” and “significant impact” unless the following sentence makes the claim concrete.
- Do not manufacture a clean linear process. Show the route the project actually took.
- Do not manufacture statistics. A precise qualitative change is stronger than an unsupported percentage.

## Content interview prompts

Before writing a project, collect short, unpolished answers to these questions:

1. What was strange, difficult, or unusually important about this project?
2. What did the brief initially get wrong or leave unresolved?
3. Which part was unmistakably yours?
4. What did you make with your own hands?
5. Who changed the work for the better, and how?
6. Where did you disagree, push back, compromise, or lose?
7. Which decision would you defend in a room of sceptics?
8. What artefact best proves that decision?
9. What changed that can be observed without inventing a metric?
10. What would you change now?
11. What detail do you remember that never appears in the official project story?

Those answers are the raw material. Write from them rather than filling a case-study template.
