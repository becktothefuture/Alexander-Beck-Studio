[FIRST READING SECTION]

[PARAGRAPH — principle followed by three examples]
What do I mean by a complex problem? The ground is always shifting. A technology such as AI or digital identity can be useful before people are ready to trust it. A new product can ask for an interaction no one has learned yet. An ordinary tool—the phone keyboard, a consent screen, a sign-in flow—can shape millions of decisions without attracting attention.

[PULL-OUT SENTENCE]
That is where I see the most potential for useful innovation.

[PARAGRAPH — includes a concrete example of useful friction]
I want to understand what sits beneath the surface: technical constraints, human behaviour, business goals and the small details that shape an experience. The answer might be to simplify, explain something differently, or add a moment of friction—a pause before someone shares sensitive information. When design works, it can disappear into the experience. That is a good thing.

[PARAGRAPH]
I started my career in Communication Design in Mainz, Germany, around thirteen years ago. Technology interested me, but visual communication showed me how a single image, word, type choice or movement can change what someone understands and feels. That still fascinates me, and it remains fundamental to how I work.

[PARAGRAPH — Yoti provides the concrete example]
In creative agencies I moved towards interface design, motion, design systems and close collaboration with developers. At Yoti in London, the challenge was more delicate: use product design and brand to help people trust digital identity. That was when I stopped treating product, brand and communication as separate disciplines. They solve different parts of the same problem, and they work best in service of one another.

[SECOND READING SECTION]

[PARAGRAPH — understanding the wider problem]
When I begin a project, I want to understand the business, the people and the space around the problem before deciding what the work should become. A brief may name the visible problem. It rarely explains the forces that created it.

[PULL-OUT SENTENCE]
Good design does not always make things easier.

[PARAGRAPH — critical position on simplicity and friction]
We live in a time of frictionless products and instant answers. I don’t believe less is always more. Sometimes design should slow someone down, make a decision visible or leave complexity intact. A confirmation before sharing an identity can be useful friction if it helps someone understand what they are doing.

[PARAGRAPH — using trends without being led by them]
I use trends when they help people enter something new. I distrust them when they replace the idea. An interface can look current and still have nothing to say. I would rather make something that belongs to the problem and lasts beyond the moment.

[PARAGRAPH — making early and protecting character through implementation]
I make things early and stay close to development. A prototype reveals where an idea is useful and where it is only decoration. Systems matter, but standardise every unusual decision and the result becomes another copy of a copy. The structure should be reliable. The important moments should still feel made by a person.

[PARAGRAPH — critical position on AI]
AI sharpens the same tension. It helps me explore further and make ideas visible sooner. It can also average them into something merely plausible. I use it to widen the search. Deciding what is worth making is still my responsibility.

[PARAGRAPH — personal projects become the examples]
Not everything starts with a client. Some projects begin with a question I can’t leave alone: could a phone keyboard work better? How should people understand AI? What makes a digital product feel trustworthy? Others lead into robotics or creative engineering.

[PARAGRAPH]
Some become things I can share; others remain sketches, prototypes or half-answered questions. All of them shape what I do next.
