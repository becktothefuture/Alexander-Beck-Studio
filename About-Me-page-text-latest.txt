[FIRST READING SECTION]

[PARAGRAPH]
What do I mean by a complex problems?   Paradigms are constanly evolving. Sometimes it’s a new technology that we don’t quite trust yet. Sometimes it’s a product that has no obvious way to use yet because no one has built it before. Sometimes it’s something completely ordinary that we experience every day without ever questioning it.

[PULL-OUT SENTENCE]
This is where I see pure potential for rational innovation.

[PARAGRAPH]
I have always liked to understand what sits underneath the surface. The technical constraints. The human mindset. The business goals. The tiny details that shape any experience.   And sometimes the answer is making something simpler. Sometimes it’s explaining it differently. Even adding friction can be helpful. Sometimes it’s explaining an idea so clearly that people forget it ever seemed complicated.   The best design often disappears into the experience. And I think that’s a really good thing.

[PARAGRAPH]
I started my career in Communication Design in Mainz, Germany, around thirteen years ago. Although I always enjoyed technology, I kept coming back to the fundamental effect that visual communication can have on people. A single image. A typographic decision. A carefully chosen word. A piece of motion. Each can completely change how someone understands an idea, or how they feel about it. That still fascinates me today and has become a fundamental part of how I work.

[PARAGRAPH — “People had to trust it” is highlighted in blue]
In creative agencies I naturally drifted towards interface design, motion, design systems and close collaboration with developers. Moving to London to join Yoti challenged me to work in service of users, using design and brand to instill trust into something as delicate as people's identities online. Is was also the moment when I stopped seeing product, brand and communication as separate disciplines. They’re all different ways of solving the same problem. And they all need to work in service of one another.

[SECOND READING SECTION]

[PARAGRAPH]
When I begin a project I tend to disappear into it for a while. I ask a lot of questions. I collect references from unexpected places. I speak to the people who understand the problem better than anyone else.

[PULL-OUT SENTENCE]
I’ve learned that the brief is often only part of the story.

[PARAGRAPH — “A rough prototype can create better conversations” is highlighted in blue]
I also like making things early. Not because they are finished, but because they reveal where everyone’s assumptions begin to fall apart. A rough prototype can create better conversations than a polished presentation.

[PARAGRAPH]
I care about giving projects a strong point of view, but I’m not precious about my ideas. Good work gets better when smart people challenge it. I also stay close to development. Partly because I genuinely enjoy building things. Mostly because I’ve seen too many good ideas lose what made them special during implementation.

[PARAGRAPH — “strengthens human judgement” is highlighted in blue]
AI has become another tool I use almost every day. It helps me think faster, prototype earlier and explore ideas I wouldn’t have had time to pursue before. It’s an incredible amplifier. It’s also incredibly good at producing work that looks convincing before anyone has asked whether it’s solving the right problem. That feels like an important distinction. I think AI becomes valuable when it strengthens human judgement, not when it replaces it.

[PARAGRAPH]
Not everything I build starts with a client. Quite often it starts with a question that refuses to leave me alone. Recently that’s included redesigning the iOS keyboard, exploring better ways for people to understand AI, thinking about trust in digital products, and experimenting with robotics and creative engineering.

[PARAGRAPH]
Some of these projects become something I can share. Others stay as sketches, prototypes or half-answered questions. They still shape how I approach whatever comes next.
